Chickweed (Stellaria media)

Season: Winter to early spring

Where to find: Lawns, garden beds, disturbed soil

Usage: Fresh in salads, as a cooked green. Mild, slightly sweet flavor.

Miner's Lettuce (Claytonia perfoliata)

Season: Late winter to spring

Where to find: Shady, moist areas

Usage: Excellent salad green. The entire plant is edible with a mild, pleasant taste.

Wild Mustard (Brassica spp.)

Season: Late winter to spring

Where to find: Fields, disturbed areas

Usage: Young leaves for salads or cooking. Seeds can be ground into mustard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to harvest these plants?

Yes! However, always make 100% positive identification before consuming any plant. When in doubt, don't eat it. Also be aware that campus landscaping may use pesticides, so wash all foraged items thoroughly.

Is there benefit to eating wild plants?

Yes, many wild plants are rich in nutrients and can provide health benefits. Industrially produced crops are selectively bred to be sweet and non-bitter, which means that many of the nutrients and antioxidants naturally produced by plants to protect themselves from pests and UV damage have been bred out. Foraging allows you to access a wider variety of plant foods that are often more nutrient-dense than store-bought options, and eating a more diverse diet is fantastic for health and longevity.

How do I make tea from these plants?

Most tea plants can be used fresh or dried. For fresh leaves/flowers, use about 1-2 tablespoons per cup of hot water and steep for 5-10 minutes. Dried material is more concentrated, so use less.

Can I harvest rosemary and other herbs year-round?

Most perennial herbs like rosemary can be harvested year-round. Just take small amounts from each plant so it can continue growing.

Why should I forage for these plants?

Foraging is a the best way to eat with a low impact. These plants are not just free and readily available, they're also devoid of packaging or emissions associated with transporting them to a vendor. Not to mention, they're much fresher than anything you could buy!

Can I report new tea/herb plants?

Yes! Please contact me at echamb@stanford.edu if you find additional map-worthy plants on campus.

About This Project

The Stanford Tea & Herbs Map is a companion to the Stanford Fruit Map, documenting the incredible variety of edible and useful plants growing on campus beyond just fruits. The map is not complete and probably never will be, but it serves as a good starting point for anyone curious about the edible bounty available here.

What You'll Find Here

This map includes perennial plants used for tea (like magnolia, osmanthus), culinary herbs (rosemary, pepper), and information about edible annual plants that appear seasonally.

Acknowledgements

I want to note my appreciation for the rich ethnobotanical knowledge held by the Muwekma Ohlone people and other Indigenous communities. These people have known which plants are good to eat and why for generations, and I feel incredibly fortunate for a chance to partake in some of these endemic plant foods. Most of the campus fruits are not native to the CA Floristic Province, but many of the edible plants that grow more wildly around campus are, and have been used for millenia as both food and medicine.
